01

Never Practice at Three Feet

In 1978, Kerr and Booth asked children to throw beanbags. For the eight year olds, the target was three feet away. One group practiced only at three feet. The other group practiced only at two feet and four feet. They never threw at three feet. After twelve weeks, both groups were tested at three feet.

The children who had never thrown at three feet hit the target better.

I do not want to push this result too far. The study had 64 children and a twelve week gym program. When a similar test was done with adults, the effect was small and did not last.

So the title of this page is not a fact. It is a question. If we only practice at the target, what do we fail to learn?

03

Don't Try to Hit the Target

Richard Sennett opens chapter eight of The Craftsman with archery. The teacher says to the student, do not try to hit the target. Wanting to hit it stops you from feeling the resistance. The resistance of the bow, of the arm, of your own habit.

For Sennett, skill is not removing resistance. It is working with it. He calls making things difficult on purpose a skill. The two feet and four feet in the experiment are that.

But not every difficulty teaches. Sennett writes that resistance which is too big, lasts too long, or cannot be explored makes people give up.

A difficulty that teaches has conditions. It is hard, but you can explore it. When you fail, information is left behind. On the next try, you can correct your judgment.

If varied practice teaches, this is why. Two feet and four feet surround three feet. The miss is visible. The next throw is always there.

04

Where Does Judgment Happen

Here, two questions need to be kept apart.

Kerr and Booth ask this. In how many conditions do you practice. One distance, or several. David Pye asks something different. While you are making it, can your hand still change the result. Or was the result fixed before you started. He calls the first the workmanship of risk. He calls the second the workmanship of certainty.

Put the two questions together and you get four boxes.

Hover a box to see what it looks like in a design studio.

Specific and Varied both throw by hand. Both are Risk in Pye's sense. So the difference between them is not whether judgment is there. It is whether the conditions were designed so that judgment could grow. Only Generate is Certainty. The hand does nothing.

And there is a fourth box. Change the conditions as much as you like. If the result arrives automatically, no judgment grows. Give a student ten sites and ten briefs. If the student generates an image every time, that is not Varied. That is Generate.

Architecture education is facing both questions at once. A teacher can vary the conditions. Whether judgment stays in the work is decided first by the tool. That is why the second question is more urgent.

05

The Architect's Three Feet

What is three feet for an architect. For me, in a wide sense, it is space as the body experiences it. But that is too wide to be a target.

Narrowed down, it is this. The ability to predict and adjust the relation between the physical conditions of a space and how a person senses them. How the height of a step meets the leg. How the height of a ceiling changes a voice. How the position of a window changes the temperature of an afternoon.

The architect does not shape the finished building by hand. The architect throws a prediction, through drawings and models, toward a sense that does not exist yet. The beanbag lands years later, in someone else's body. So for the architect, the throw is hard to practice from the start.

Here we need to be careful. This page does not say do not practice at the target. It says do not practice only in the same condition as the target. Architects need to go to buildings. But if we design only from spaces we already know, senses we are used to, and a body that is always the same, the judgment that adjusts when conditions change does not grow.

Two and four feet for an architect might look like this. Instead of memorizing a standard ceiling height, or generating a beautiful ceiling with AI, a student works at both ends. A ceiling at 1.8 meters, where the breath gets short. A ceiling at 6 meters, where the voice scatters. Three feet, the ceiling the body wants, is never given. It is found between the two.

Then how should we look at the tools. There was a time when architecture professors criticized students for using CAD and rendering instead of drawing by hand. 3D modeling and rendering are not bad because they are digital. You can change the angle of light, the material, the ceiling height, and study the relation. That is Varied. Digital tools become Generate when they are used to drop the result in front of you.

AI images are faster. So the question is more urgent too. When the result is made for you, what judgment is left for the learner.

The question is not what the tool is. It is what decisions the student has to make while using it.

06

What Should Remain Difficult

As someone who is interested in VR and wants to add multisensory experience to digital space, I want to ask myself a critical question. Adding senses to a digital space may be neither two feet nor four feet. It may only make the target easier to see. The throwing distance stays the same.

VR can remove resistance. It can also build resistance that the real world cannot. So the question is not the order or the kind of media. It is which resistance to keep in this space, and which resistance to add.

Sennett's chapter has a second word next to resistance. Ambiguity. A beanbag has one target. A space does not. The relation between a ceiling and a voice, a window and an afternoon, never settles into one right answer. That ambiguity is not a defect of architecture. It is where people improvise, like the front steps that became a living room. Generate does not only remove the resistance. It flattens the ambiguity too, into one smooth, finished image.

What does it mean to teach architectural design? At least for me, better design education is designing the difficulties that students will meet. When, how much, and with what feedback.

Back to the throw. Varied is practice where someone designed the difficulty. Two and four were chosen to surround three. Specific is practice where the difficulty was left alone. Generate is practice where the difficulty was cleared away. I feel the tools of architecture education are moving from Specific to Generate. The direction I think we should take is from Specific to Varied.

Does the tool I make help students succeed more easily. Or does it help them learn to judge for themselves.

If a tool removes every difficulty, what is left for the student to learn?
